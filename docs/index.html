Predicting CTR Growth: A Decision-Support Model for Content Review Prioritization

Abstract. Content strategists cannot manually review every page for click-through-rate (CTR) opportunity, so we ask: can a page's search performance over one 30-day window predict whether its CTR will meaningfully grow in the next 30 days? Using ~107,000 anonymized content items from FlyRank's search-performance warehouse, we built a random forest classifier validated with client-grouped splits, compared against a transparent hand-written baseline rule. The model reaches a Precision@50 of 0.320 against a base rate of 0.225 — a 1.42x lift over random ranking — while the baseline rule performs below the base rate The output is a ranked, reason-coded review queue meant to prioritize a human strategist's attention, not to automate content decisions.

1Introduction / Problem

Content that ranks well in search quietly loses click-through rate over time — rankings hold, but fewer searchers click. Most teams notice this only after the fact. Given thousands of pages and limited editorial time, the practical question isn't "is this page declining?" in the abstract — it's which page should a strategist look at first?

This project ranks pages by their likelihood of CTR growth over the next 30 days, so review time goes to the pages most worth a second look. A wrong recommendation costs wasted editorial time, not a dangerous outcome — which is why this is framed throughout as decision-support: a ranked shortlist for a human to check, never an automated action.

2Data

Release: FlyRank/internship-warehouse (Hugging Face), build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703.

Tables used:

  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily search performance, used to build two consecutive 30-day windows per content item.
  • dim_content — content metadata (word count, content type, search intent, competition level) joined in as model features.

Date window: the most recent 60 days of the daily fact table (relative to its latest date), split into a "prev30" window and a "last30" window.

Rows: 107,324 content items had at least 50 impressions in both windows. This volume floor was applied first, so pages with near-zero traffic couldn't produce meaningless CTR swings that look dramatic in percentage terms but represent one or two clicks.

Excluded, and why:

  • content_updated_date, last_optimized_date, optimization_eligible_date — not verified to fall safely before the feature window, so including them risked leaking information about edits made during the period being predicted.
  • fact_content_query_90d — its 90-day window overlaps the most recent months of the snapshot, which would overlap the label window. Left out entirely.
  • No raw URLs, client names, domains, or keyword text appear anywhere in this analysis — only hashed client_hash_id / content_hash_id, used solely for grouping and joining, never as model inputs.

3Methodology

Label definition

is_ctr_growing = 1 when CTR in the "last30" window is at least 0.3 percentage points higher than CTR in the "prev30" window, else 0.

A leakage artifact we caught and fixed. An earlier version of this label used a relative threshold ("CTR grew by 20%"). That version produced a suspicious Precision@50 of 0.98 — investigating showed the model had simply learned "if starting CTR is near zero, predict growth," since a tiny absolute click gain looks enormous in percentage terms when the starting point is near zero. Switching to an absolute point-gain threshold removed that shortcut. We're reporting this because catching it mattered more to the integrity of this result than the number itself.

Features

Numeric: impressions and clicks in the prev30 window, prev30 CTR, average search position, word count, character count, search volume, keyword competition, cost-per-click, backlink count.

Categorical: content type, main search intent, competition level.

Excluded as leakage: impressions, clicks, and CTR from the last30 window — these are what the label is directly computed from.

Baseline

A transparent rule scoring pages by how close to page-1 position they already are, how far their current CTR sits below this dataset's own median CTR, and how much traffic they carry. The "weak CTR" cutoff was set to this dataset's measured median, not an arbitrary borrowed number — an earlier draft used a fixed threshold from a different dataset's scale and produced a baseline that wasn't meaningfully discriminating between pages at all.

Validation design

GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client_hash_id, 80/20 train/test. This guarantees no client's pages appear in both train and test — a random row-level split would let the model partly memorize per-client patterns and overstate real-world performance.

Leakage checks

An automated assertion halts the pipeline if any last30-derived column appears in the feature list. Feature importances were inspected after training to confirm no single feature suspiciously dominates the model's decisions.

5Limitations & Honest Framing

  • Observed, not causal. This model finds pages statistically associated with future CTR growth. It does not identify what to change on a page, and cannot claim that any specific edit causes growth — that would require a controlled experiment (before/after a real edit, compared against a held-out control group).
  • One snapshot, one pair of windows. The label covers a single 60-day slice of a 17-month history. Whether this pattern holds across other periods — different seasons, different search-result layouts — is untested here.
  • Threshold choices are ours, not the data's. The 0.3-point CTR growth threshold and the 50-impression volume floor were deliberate choices. A different threshold would describe a different rate of "success."
  • Some content metadata is incomplete. Fields like word count and backlink count are missing for a share of rows and were filled with zero rather than dropped, which could understate their real importance to the model.
  • Decision-support only. The ranked output is meant to prioritize human review. It is not, and should not be treated as, an automated publishing decision.

6Ranked Recommendations

The model produces a ranked queue of content items ordered by predicted probability of CTR growth. Each row carries reason codes and a suggested action:

  • review_title_and_meta — currently below-median CTR for its position, and the model flags it as a likely grower. The clearest candidate for a title or meta-description rewrite.
  • monitor_closely — model flags growth likelihood without the CTR-gap signal present.
  • monitor — no strong signal either way; lower priority for manual review right now.

Intended use: a strategist works down the list starting at rank 1, checking each page against editorial judgment before making any change. This list is a starting point for a human decision, not a replacement for one.

What should never be automated: automatically rewriting or republishing content based on this score alone. The model has no visibility into brand voice, factual accuracy, or business priorities — a human review step is required for every recommended action.
